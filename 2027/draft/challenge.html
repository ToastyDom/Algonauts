Challenge overview

Understanding how the human brain works is one of the key challenges that science and society face. The Algonauts challenge proposes an open and quantitative test of how well computational models do today. The 2027 challenge targets brain responses during active video game play, where subjects’ decisions and actions shape what they see and hear next.

We provide gameplay video, audio, controller actions and annotated game events from four retro video games, together with the corresponding fMRI responses of six subjects. Challenge participants build encoding models that predict brain responses for held-out gameplay from these games (in-distribution, ID) and for previously unseen games (out-of-distribution, OOD), for which the brain data are withheld. Submissions are scored on CodaBench.

Development Phase. Challenge participants train their encoding models on gameplay and fMRI responses from all four games, and test them in-distribution on held-out gameplay: selected levels of Super Mario Bros. 3 and selected runs of Super Mario All-Stars. The gameplay of these is released, while the fMRI responses are withheld. A public leaderboard gives feedback on submissions.

Generalization Phase. At the beginning of this phase, we release about two hours of gameplay per subject from games that are not part of the development data, and withhold the corresponding fMRI responses. The identities of these games are not revealed before the phase begins. They were selected to differ from the Mario-family games in visual appearance, behavioral demands and cognitive demands. Performance in this phase determines the final ranking.

Post-Challenge Benchmark. Once the challenge is over, both leaderboards remain open indefinitely as public benchmarks for ID and OOD generalization.

Evaluation

After each submission, the scoring program correlates (Pearson’s r) the predicted and withheld fMRI responses for each of the 1,134 brain parcels, separately for each subject and game. Predictions are joined across all evaluated gameplay recordings into one continuous sequence, so longer recordings contribute more time points. These correlation scores are averaged first across parcels, then across subjects, and finally across games with equal weight, resulting in a single score for each submission.

Five subjects are evaluated: sub-01, sub-02, sub-03, sub-05 and sub-06. Subject sub-04, who only played Shinobi III, is not evaluated; its data are provided as additional training data.

Challenge rules

  1. Challenge participants can use any encoding model, pretrained representation or external training data. Using the withheld fMRI responses of the test data is prohibited.
  2. The winners are determined by performance in predicting fMRI responses for the unseen games during the Generalization Phase.
  3. Teams can make unlimited submissions during the Development Phase and up to ten during the Generalization Phase. Each team can only compete using one account; creating multiple accounts results in disqualification.
  4. To be considered for the final ranking, teams submit a short report describing their encoding model. The top three teams must make their code publicly available and are invited to present their approach at CCN 2027 in Edinburgh.

Development Kit

To facilitate participation, we provide a Python Development Kit as an interactive notebook, which runs on Google Colab or locally in Jupyter. It accompanies users through the challenge in five steps:

  1. Setup: install the required libraries and access the tutorial data.
  2. Data familiarization: explore gameplay recordings, controller actions, game events and fMRI responses.
  3. Feature extraction: extract visual, audio, controller-action and game-event features.
  4. Encoding model: train and test an fMRI encoding model.
  5. Submission: prepare predicted fMRI responses for CodaBench.

Baseline model

The challenge baseline is a simple multimodal encoding model and an accessible starting point, not a restriction on your methods. Features from four stimulus modalities are aligned to the 1.49-second fMRI sampling grid, combined across a window of recent gameplay shifted for the hemodynamic delay, and mapped onto each of the 1,134 parcels with ridge regression. How to predict brain data?

We put close to no restrictions on how you predict brain data (see Rules). A commonly used approach is the linearizing encoding model, where the response of each brain parcel is independently predicted from stimulus features of a computational model, typically with linear regression (Wu et al., 2006; Naselaris et al., 2011; Kriegeskorte & Douglas, 2019). The Development Kit implements such a model.
