Challenge motivation

Understanding how the human brain works is one of the key challenges that science and society face. The Algonauts challenge proposes a test of how well computational models do today. This test is intrinsically open and quantitative. This will allow us to precisely assess the progress in explaining the human brain.

In a video game, perception and action form a closed loop: what players see and hear guides their decisions and actions, and those actions shape the sensory input they receive next. The primary target of the 2027 Challenge is predicting human brain responses during this active interaction.

We pose the question: How well does your computational model account for brain activity during gameplay?

Challenge overview

The goal of the 2027 challenge is to provide a platform for biological and artificial intelligence scientists to cooperate and compete in developing cutting-edge brain encoding models. Specifically, these models should predict brain responses during video gameplay, and generalize outside their training distribution.

In collaboration with the Courtois NeuroMod Project (CNeuroMod), we provide:

  • gameplay video, audio, controller actions and game events from four retro video games,
  • the corresponding fMRI responses for the three model training games, and
  • a Development Kit with a video tutorial to help you get familiar with the data.

With that, challenge participants are expected to build computational models that predict brain responses during gameplay for which the brain data are withheld.

How well does a model trained on three games predict brain responses while subjects play other games? To answer this question, we test in-distribution (ID) generalization on the visually enhanced remake of Super Mario Bros. in Super Mario All-Stars, which preserves the original levels and basic actions. We test out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization on a separate set of hidden games.

Challenge participants submit predicted fMRI responses in the format described in the Development Kit to the challenge platform, Codabench, for automatic scoring (see Evaluation and submission).

Challenge phases

The challenge has two competitive phases. During the Development Phase, participants compare models on the in-distribution (ID) test game. During the Generalization Phase, prediction accuracy on the out-of-distribution (OOD) games determines the final ranking. The two tests remain available after the competition in the Post-Challenge Phase.

Development Phase (6 months)

Model training. The three model training games are Super Mario Bros., Super Mario Bros. 3 and Shinobi III: Return of the Ninja Master. We provide approximately 144 hours of gameplay data and the corresponding fMRI responses for model training.

ID testing. We use the visually enhanced remake of Super Mario Bros. from Super Mario All-Stars, a collection of four Mario remakes. This remake preserves the original levels and basic actions. We release approximately 30 hours of gameplay data across all 22 recorded levels and five test subjects. The corresponding fMRI responses remain hidden. Participants submit predicted fMRI responses to Codabench, where they are scored against the hidden responses. Participants can use their ID scores to compare model variants during this phase.

Generalization Phase (1 week)

OOD testing. At the start of this phase, we reveal the OOD games and release approximately two hours of gameplay data per test subject. We provide the same gameplay data modalities as during the Development Phase and keep the corresponding fMRI responses hidden. Withholding the game identities until this phase begins limits game-specific preparation.

Participants submit predicted fMRI responses for the OOD games to Codabench. Game scores are averaged within each subject, weighting games equally, and then averaged across subjects. Only OOD prediction accuracy determines the final ranking among eligible teams. The Evaluation and submission section describes the scoring procedure.

Post-Challenge Phase (indefinite)

Following the competitive phases, the ID and OOD tests remain open as public benchmarks. Participants can submit predictions for the same test gameplay data to compare future models with submissions from the official challenge. This way, the challenge can keep supporting better models of brain activity during gameplay long after the competition ends. The corresponding fMRI responses remain hidden.

Evaluation and submission

Codabench automatically evaluates model predictions by comparing the predicted fMRI time series with the corresponding withheld measurements. ID testing uses all recorded levels from Super Mario All-Stars, comprising approximately 30 hours of gameplay across subjects. OOD testing uses approximately two hours of gameplay per subject. Both test sets contain data from sub-01, sub-02, sub-03, sub-05 and sub-06.

For each subject and game, the predicted and measured fMRI time series are concatenated across all corresponding gameplay videos in the test set. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) is then calculated between the predicted and measured sequences separately for each of the 1,134 brain parcels.

The parcel correlations are averaged to obtain one score per subject and game. For the OOD test set, which contains multiple games, game scores are averaged within each subject, weighting games equally. The scores are then averaged across subjects to obtain a single prediction accuracy score per submission. The ID scores determine submission rankings on the public leaderboard during the Development Phase, whereas the OOD scores determine the final challenge ranking.

Prepare a submission

For the five test subjects, submit predicted fMRI responses corresponding to gameplay. Use the Super Mario All-Stars gameplay data during the Development Phase and the OOD gameplay data during the Generalization Phase.

The Challenge submission section of the Development Kit will show how to prepare the predictions for upload to Codabench.

Validate before submitting (optional)

Before submitting predictions, you can divide the released model training data into training and validation partitions. Fit the model using only the training partition, then compare its predictions with the recorded fMRI responses in the validation partition to assess model choices.

To check cross-game generalization before the Generalization Phase, you can train on Super Mario Bros. and Super Mario Bros. 3 and reserve Shinobi III for validation. This tests transfer beyond the related Mario games to a game with different sensory content, actions and game events. This split is available for sub-01, sub-02 and sub-06, who have recordings from both the Mario games and Shinobi III.

This validation is something you run yourself on the released model training data. It is separate from the official ID and OOD test sets, whose fMRI responses stay hidden and which are scored only on Codabench.

Rules and regulations

  1. Challenge participants can use any encoding model derived from any source and may use external training data. However, training models on any neural responses corresponding to the ID or OOD test gameplay data is prohibited.
  2. Each team can make an unlimited number of submissions during the Development Phase and a maximum of ten submissions during the Generalization Phase. Each team can only compete using one account.

    Violation of either rule 1 or rule 2 will result in disqualification from the challenge.

  3. To promote open science, challenge participants who wish to be considered for the final ranking must publish a short report (approximately 4–8 pages) describing their encoding model and methodology on a preprint server (e.g., arXiv or bioRxiv). Participants must send the preprint link to the organizers by filling out the form below by June 30, 2027.
  4. The three highest-ranked teams must make their code openly available and submit the link to the organizers using the same form by June 30, 2027. In the spirit of open science, all teams are encouraged to make their code openly available and link to it in their reports.

Development Kit

The Development Kit is an interactive Python notebook that runs on Google Colab or locally in Jupyter. It introduces the challenge data, walks through the baseline encoding model, and explains how to prepare a submission.

  1. Setup: install the required libraries and access the tutorial data.
  2. Data familiarization: explore gameplay recordings, controller actions, game events and fMRI responses.
  3. Feature extraction: extract visual, audio, controller-action and game-event features.
  4. Encoding model training: train and test an fMRI encoding model using the supplied features.
  5. Challenge submission: prepare and format predicted fMRI responses for submission to Codabench.

Baseline model

We provide a challenge baseline encoding model as a working example and a reference point for prediction accuracy. Participants may use their own features, model architectures and training strategies.

The baseline extracts gameplay features at each fMRI sampling interval (repetition time, TR = 1.49 s). To account for the delay in the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) response, the model uses features from four consecutive intervals, the most recent of which precedes the response by three TRs (4.47 s). It concatenates these features and uses ridge regression to predict the response of each brain parcel. On the in-distribution test game, Super Mario All-Stars, the baseline achieved a prediction score of r = 0.209.

Gameplay dataFeatures
VideoActivations from the final pooling layer of the pretrained Slow R50 video network, projected onto the first 100 principal components.
AudioMel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), averaged within each TR and projected onto the first 10 principal components.
Controller actionsFraction of each TR for which each controller action is held, using nine action labels.
Game eventsA 384-dimensional embedding represents each event description. Within each TR, the model sums the embeddings weighted by event activity and appends total event activity as the 385th feature.

Encoding models for fMRI prediction

In the 2027 challenge, we leave it up to the challenge participants to determine the approach to predict brain responses (see Rules). As an example, we describe one common approach called linearizing encoding (Wu et al., 2006; Kay et al., 2008; Naselaris et al., 2011; Yamins & DiCarlo, 2016; Kriegeskorte & Douglas, 2019), where the response of each brain parcel is independently predicted from features extracted by a computational model, typically using linear regression. The Development Kit illustrates the three steps below.

  1. Feature extraction. Convert gameplay video, audio, controller actions and game event annotations from the model training games into numerical features. The features of a given model are interpreted as a hypothesis about the features that the brain uses to represent the gameplay.
  2. Model training. Use the paired gameplay features and measured fMRI responses to fit a linear mapping for each brain parcel. Each parcel’s response is modeled as a weighted combination of multiple features. The challenge baseline model uses ridge regression.
  3. Model testing. Apply the trained mapping to features from the test gameplay data to predict the corresponding fMRI responses. If the computational model is a suitable model of the brain, its predictions will fit the recorded data well.
